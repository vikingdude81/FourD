Personas Are Not People: Identity as State, Not Attribute
TAMUNOTONYE HARRY, University of Vermont, USA
YUANYUAN FENG, University of Vermont, USA
Synthetic populations, large sets of simulated people, are increasingly used in place of real people to pretest surveys, simulate users and
communities, and evaluate AI systems. Each simulated person is defined by a persona, a written profile of fixed traits, demographics,
and attitudes that a language model is instructed to act out. Every such system assumes that a person’s identity is a stable attribute
that behaves the same way in every situation the simulation presents. We show that this assumption is not a harmless simplification.
Three decades of personality research find the opposite. How a given person behaves varies more across contexts than people vary
from one another. Drawing on identity economics, which describes how different situations activate different identities and norms
within the same person, we separate behavior into three parts, which are stable traits, the identity a context calls up, and the situation
itself. We then prove, under two stated assumptions, that any simulation built on fixed personas, no matter how detailed the personas
or how large the underlying model, cannot reproduce the part of a population’s behavior that is specific to it and its circumstances. We
propose a new evaluation criterion based on this result and a person-in-context design that can meet it in principle. We test one version
on a naturalistic Reddit personality corpus and on a published clinical-trial recruitment survey of a community with documented
distrust of medical institutions. The design does produce context-dependent behavior when tested directly, but in neither setting does
it fully match the real population, and we show where it falls short. The consequence for practice is a risk that synthetic-population
evaluations misrepresent the groups whose behavior is most shaped by context, a distortion that richer personas cannot correct.
Additional Key Words and Phrases: synthetic populations, LLM personas, AI evaluation, within-person variance, context-dependent
behavior, identity, representational harm, personality psychology, simulation of human behavior

Introduction
Companies and researchers now pretest surveys, products, and messages on AI-simulated populations before asking real
customers or citizens, and some propose using the simulations instead of asking at all [4, 8, 21]. Some systems already
hold billions of simulated people [21], built in part from hundreds of hours of real interviews [25]. These systems share
one design choice. Each simulated person is a persona, a written profile, vector, or interview transcript that is prepared
before the simulation runs. Some systems retrieve different parts of it for different questions, but what is stored about
the person never changes. A persona therefore fixes the person’s identity too, which we take from Akerlof and Kranton
[1] to mean a person’s sense of self as a member of a social category, along with the norms for how members of that
category act. A real person holds several identities, such as employee, parent, or friend, and which one is active depends
on the situation, so the same person may be formal and careful in a meeting with colleagues and relaxed and joking at
dinner with friends (§3.1). Current persona designs cannot represent this shift. Any change in behavior across situations
is the large language model (LLM) improvising, and the persona itself never changes (Figure 1).
In personality psychology, most of the variation in trait-related behavior is within persons rather than between them
[11, 12]. Identity economics [1, 2] offers an account of this variation. A person belongs to several social categories, each
category carries norms for how its members act, and the situation determines which category is active and therefore
which norms the person follows (§2.2). Recent work has started to notice the symptoms. Studies report that fixed
personas collapse toward a typical default [23] and compress variation within demographic groups [39], while other
work lets the personality of a single dialogue agent shift as a conversation unfolds [27, 40]. To our knowledge, none of

The gap has consequences. Suppose a city emergency office pretests the wording of a flood evacuation alert on
simulated residents, because a live test is impossible. A real resident may ignore a text from the city as one more
automated message, yet leave at once when a neighbor knocks on the door or a relative calls. A persona that says
“distrusts authorities” predicts that no version of the alert works, and a persona that leaves distrust out predicts that
every version works. Either way, the office chooses its wording and its messenger on the model’s guess, and the residents
who most need to leave may stay. The error matters more when the simulated people stand in for patients or citizens
and the decision reaches a real community (§6.3).
We make three contributions.
(1) Can richer personas fix this? No, and we give a formal proof. Any simulation built on a fixed persona cannot
reproduce what is specific to a person beyond their group’s typical response, however detailed the persona and
however large the model. The proof relies on two stated assumptions, and we test how it degrades when they only
roughly hold (§4.2, Appendix B).

(2) How would we know? A new evaluation criterion. Latent State-Trait (LST) fingerprint recovery has three parts. It
asks how much of the variation in behavior is between people, how much comes from particular people in particular
situations, and whether a simulation tracks each person’s specific response. The proof shows that, in principle, fixed
personas cannot meet the third check (§4).
(3) What would work? A person-in-context design and two tests of it. We build a person-in-context approach,
in which a situation selects an identity state that the language model then acts out, and we test it against fixed
personas on two real datasets. Fixed personas make each person behave almost the same in every situation, far more
than real people do, and our design comes closer on all five personality traits we tested. On a published clinical-trial
recruitment study, our design reproduces the overall effect of trust on how respondents rate the ads, and its behavior
changes as expected when the identity state is switched. It does not reproduce the study’s central finding that
which ad theme works best depends on how much a person trusts the healthcare system, and we explain why in §5.
Person-specific signal is near zero for every system we test, so that test cannot yet separate them.
In principle, a person-in-context design can represent person-specific response to context, which a fixed persona
provably cannot. In our tests, the implementation we built reproduces some features of the real data, such as the effect
of trust, but not yet the finding that the best ad theme depends on trust. Closing that gap is an open problem.
2
Related Work
Our contribution connects three bodies of work that have not been brought together, namely synthetic populations for
AI evaluation, behavioral science on how people act in context, and the critique of representational harm in generative
systems.
2.1
Synthetic populations for AI evaluation
Simulated people are used for LLM-based evaluation in many ways, for example to pretest survey questions, to simulate
public opinion, and to stress-test chat systems with simulated users. These systems fall into three families, which differ
in how they represent identity.
The fixed-persona family dominates current systems. A persona, whether a categorical vector, a written profile, or an
interview transcript, is set up in advance and does not change with the situation. MatrAIx [21] is the current large-scale
example, with 8.3 billion persona records, a 1,290-dimension schema, and a 91.5% persona-adherence rate. Park et al.
[25] use two-hour interview transcripts as personas for 1,052 people, and report that, on held-out General Social Survey
items, interview-based agents reach 83% of participants’ own test-retest consistency. Argyle et al. [4] condition on
demographics with lighter personas. Bisbee et al. [8] find that such synthetic survey responses have much less variance
than real respondents, and that many regression coefficients differ significantly from human data. In this family, context
stays outside the persona, and the language model alone supplies any adaptation.
The memory-evolving family adds accumulated experience to a fixed persona. Generative Agents [24], SOTOPIA [45],
Concordia [37], OASIS [43], and AgentSociety [26] give agents a memory stream with reflection or planning. Variation
then follows what has happened to the agent, closer to a diary than to identities that the present situation brings out.
The person-in-context family treats identity as a state that context activates. It has theoretical roots in self-categorization
theory and multiple-self-aspects frameworks [22], and related computational work exists for single dialogue agents.
Pielage et al. [27] model an assistant’s personality as latent states in a state machine whose transitions follow the
conversation. Wei et al. [40] steer persona-related neurons in a model according to the situation. Lee et al. [19] build a

2.2
Behavioral science on person-in-context
In personality research, most of the variation in trait-related behavior is within persons, from one moment to the next
[12]. This helped resolve the person-situation debate of the 1990s [11, 13]. Latent State-Trait (LST) theory [31, 32] splits
behavior into trait, state, and error, and variance ratios akin to intraclass correlations give each part’s share. Whole
Trait Theory, which came later, describes a trait as a distribution of states around a stable level [11, 13]. Inspired by
both, we write behavior as 𝑌= 𝜏+ 𝑠(𝑋) + 𝜀, a stable level plus a response to the situation plus noise. In §3.1 we extend
𝑠(𝑋) with a person-specific, context-activated term 𝐼𝑝,𝑐,𝑡.
One study applies these methods to language-model behavior directly [18]. In a Reddit corpus, it finds that most
variance is within persons, about 72 to 74% across the 26 trait dimensions it measures, without removing measurement
noise, in line with the psychological literature. It also finds that language models prompted with personas do not track
context-driven change, which it calls state-blind. Han et al. [16] also use Whole Trait Theory to read LLM agents and
find that one personality prompt is expressed differently across four conversational settings.
Identity as a building block. Akerlof and Kranton [1, 2] treat identity as a variable in a person’s decisions, with actions
judged against the norms of the identity that is active. Trait Activation Theory [35] reaches a compatible view, that
situations activate traits through cues, and self-categorization theory [36] and the multiple-self-aspects framework [22]
agree. We found no synthetic-population system or evaluation built on any of them.
2.3
Representational harm in generative systems
Representational harm connects this work to questions of fairness and accountability. A synthetic population that
flattens a group misrepresents that group, and decisions made from it reach real communities. Bender et al. [7] warn
that scaling language models without attention to whose language is represented risks amplifying dominant viewpoints.
Blodgett et al. [9] argue that work on “bias” often does not say which harms to which communities it addresses. Our
criterion responds to this by naming one specific distortion, flattening of context-dependent behavior, and measuring it.
Wang et al. [38] show that replacing human participants with LLMs can misportray and flatten identity groups. Two
findings concern persona-based systems directly. Xiao et al. [42] find that models with the highest per-persona fidelity
produce the most polarized populations, so persona adherence and population fidelity can pull apart. Batzner et al.
[6] find that only 35% of 63 persona-based LLM studies report anything about representativeness. That is a separate
problem, since a representative, transparently sampled set of personas is still fixed and would still fail our criterion.
These critiques name the outcome but not its cause. We locate the flattening in one design choice, identity as a fixed
attribute of the persona, which cannot match the real structure of within-person variation. Richer personas do not
remove it.

2.4
Gap
Behavioral science models how people act in context, and identity economics accounts for the mechanism. Neither
has been built into a synthetic-population system and tested there against real within-person variation, and current
validation does not check for it. We add what the critique literature lacks (§2.1, §2.3), namely an explanation of why
these failures occur, a new evaluation criterion derived from it (§4), and a formal result (§4.2) that, under two stated
assumptions, the failure comes from the design, whatever the scale or prompting.
3
Framework: Person-in-Context Simulation
We split behavior into three parts, which are a stable attribute of the person, an identity state that the situation brings
out, and the situation itself.

3.2
Kinds of Systems and Their Interface
A simulation system stores a representation of each person, builds a prompt for each situation, and calls a language
model. The inputs that the model’s generation call receives are the system’s interface. A single-call system produces each
response with one such call. A system with subroutines runs extra steps first, such as a rule or a second language-model
call that scores which identity a situation brings out, and passes the result into the interface. The interface is then the
full list of inputs to the generation call, whether they come from storage or from a subroutine. In the appendix, the
generation function 𝑓LLM also carries independent sampling noise 𝜂𝑝,𝑐(random variation in the generated text, so the
same input can give different responses on different calls), and the time index is dropped, since the propositions concern
the person-context decomposition only.
We call each design a family and each implementation a system. The families differ in which inputs reach the model,
whatever the number of calls.
• Fixed-persona family. The system stores one persona 𝜋𝑝that never changes, so the interface is (𝜋𝑝,𝑋𝑐,𝑡) and
only the situation changes with context. Any identity-like adaptation comes from the language model’s own
inference. MatrAIx [21], Park et al. [25], and Argyle et al. [4] are in this family.
• Memory-evolving family. The system stores a persona and a growing record of past interactions 𝑀𝑝,𝑡, so the
interface is (𝜋𝑝, 𝑀𝑝,𝑡,𝑋𝑐,𝑡) [24, 26, 37, 45]. The record grows with time and is not indexed by context. The record
encodes the agent’s history.
• Person-in-context family. The system stores a stable attribute vector 𝜏𝑝and a set I𝑝of identity states available
to the person. A subroutine 𝜋𝐼selects which state to activate, written 𝐼𝑝,𝑐,𝑡∼𝜋𝐼(𝜏𝑝,𝑋𝑐,𝑡). The interface is
(𝜏𝑝, 𝐼𝑝,𝑐,𝑡,𝑋𝑐,𝑡). The set is fixed for each person, and only the realized state changes with context. This is the
design we propose, and we know of no production system that uses it.
Adding subroutines to a fixed-persona system does not change its family if every subroutine reads only 𝜋𝑝and 𝑋𝑐,𝑡.
Its output is then still a function of (𝜋𝑝,𝑋𝑐,𝑡). What matters for the propositions is whether any interface input is both
specific to the person and changes with context for that person. Only the person-in-context family has one.
Building I𝑝from data (§5.2, §5.3) showed that the set can also include a baseline state, a default that a person returns
to when no context is strong, like the center of a person’s state distribution in Whole Trait Theory. Whether the
set has a baseline is an empirical question. The Chameleon set has one baseline and five activated states, and the
motivational-themes set has no baseline and six activated states.
3.3
A Limit Built Into the Fixed-Persona Design
We do not claim that fixed-persona and memory-evolving systems fail at what they were built for. Sampling varied
profiles for product testing [21], simulating social dynamics in a sandbox [24], and predicting individual attitudes [25]
are legitimate goals, judged fairly by their own criteria. Our claim is narrower. These systems are now proposed for
behavioral simulation, which means reproducing how a population responds across contexts. For that goal they meet a
target their design does not represent.
In a fixed-persona system the persona does not change with context, so no input says which identity the situation
has brought out for this person. The model must infer one from the persona and the situation, or a subroutine supplies
one that reads only those two inputs. Either way the output is a function of (𝜋𝑝,𝑋𝑐,𝑡) alone (Proposition 1, §4.2), so
person-context interaction is limited by the variance that the model’s own inference adds [16, 18]. This holds for every
fixed-persona system, whatever the persona format or the model. A richer persona adds between-person variance and

The person-in-context family can represent person-specific response to context by construction. Whether a given

one implementation (our specification of the identity states and how they are activated). They do not change what the
design itself can represent (§6.1).
4
Evaluation Criterion: LST Fingerprint Recovery
§3 shows that only the person-in-context family can represent 𝐼𝑝,𝑐,𝑡as person-specific. A new criterion turns that
difference into something measurable, which we call Latent State-Trait (LST) fingerprint recovery. A fingerprint is a
pattern specific enough to identify who it belongs to. A population’s LST fingerprint is how stable people are relative
to one another, and how much and in what way each person varies across situations. The criterion, built on the LST
variance decomposition (§3.1) [10, 31], checks whether a synthetic population reproduces the real fingerprint as well as
the average response levels.
4.1
The Criterion
LST fingerprint recovery compares a synthetic system A with a real population on three components.
(1) Between-person share (ICC). The intraclass correlation (ICC, bias-corrected) is the fraction of all variation in
behavior that comes from stable differences between people. The rest is one person varying across situations,
plus noise. A system that inflates this share treats identity as more fixed than real behavior supports.
(2) Person-context interaction variance (Var𝑃×𝐶). This is the variance left after removing person and context main
effects. It comes from particular people in particular contexts (§3.1). A system can match the real ICC and still
show almost no interaction, because everyone reacts to context in the same way. ICC alone cannot tell these
cases apart.
(3) Person-specific signal (person-specific residual correlation). This is the component our proof addresses. A fixed
persona cannot reach it by design, and a person-in-context system can in principle. Two people of the same
general type, for example both skeptical of institutions, still react differently to a given situation. The shared,
predictable part is state-typical. The leftover part is person-specific, the signature a system must capture if it
claims to simulate that person, beyond their type.

approximately. The prediction can also be tested. If personas leaked person-specific information, Arm A’s correlation
with the real residual would be large, and in §5.2 it is small (0.026).
What this establishes. No fixed-persona system that meets the two assumptions can reach the third component
beyond a small margin set by the corollary, whatever the model scale or data. Richer prompts or larger models can
improve the first two components without touching this limit.
4.3
Scope: What the Criterion Does Not Establish
The criterion has two limits. First, the proof concerns the third component only, so it does not say that fixed personas
must show inflated ICC or near-zero interaction variance. Those are diagnostics that we report for every system in §5.2.

A fixed-persona system could show real-scale ICC or substantial interaction variance and still fail the third component.
Second, the result depends on the two assumptions, or their approximate version. They are claims about how personas
are built and how contexts are assigned, and neither follows from context-invariance alone. A persona built from a
demographic profile and a stated identity state meets the Key Assumption directly, because Δ𝑝,𝑐is by construction
what such instruments cannot see. A persona built by hand-picking a person’s own responses across contexts would
break it, because the persona would then contain the signal that Proposition 2 assumes it lacks.
Within this scope, the criterion gives person-in-context systems a target that fixed-persona systems cannot meet in
principle, and gives any implementation of 𝜋𝐼a concrete three-part measure. §5.2 and §5.3 apply it to two datasets.
5
Empirical Tests of the Criterion
The proof in §4.2 concerns what the fixed-persona design can represent. The experiments ask what the data show about
fixed personas and what Arm C, a first, theory-motivated implementation of the alternative, achieves. In Chameleon,
fixed personas (Arms A and B) place almost all variation between people, where real people show almost none, and in
the motivational-themes study their agreement with the published pattern changes from 5 of 6 directions to 0 of 6 when
one sentence is added. Arm C comes closest to the real variance structure on every Chameleon trait, it reproduces the
effect of trust in the study, and its behavior follows the identity state it is given. Person-specific signal is near zero for
every system we test, and Arm C does not yet reproduce the study’s interaction between ad theme and trust. We trace
that gap to how the implementation represents trust, as a single level that does not change with context (§5.3.3), which
is the flattening that §6.3 names as a risk.
We chose the two datasets for what each can test. Chameleon records three posts from each of 1,667 people in
different subreddits. Its repeated measures within each person, scored on many trait dimensions in a naturalistic corpus,
make it the direct test of the between-person share and of person-specific signal. The motivational-themes study is
not a dataset in the usual sense. It is a published survey experiment with one rating set per person, and we use its
reported coefficients, sample demographics, and survey wording as a target. Because the real effect is known, we can
say precisely where a simulation departs from it. Where the difference does not matter, we call both of them datasets.
5.1
Experimental Setup
Each dataset uses the same three arms, which differ only in what the language model is told about the person. Arm A
(fixed persona) states a trait of the person once, such as “skeptical of institutions”, whatever the context. Arm B (fixed
persona plus context) states the same trait and adds a neutral mention of the context, which tests whether naming the
situation is enough when the person does not change. Arm C (person-in-context) states no fixed trait. It computes an
identity state for each (person, context) pair, and the model acts out that state. Arms A and B are fixed-persona systems
and neither keeps a record of past interactions, so neither dataset tests the memory-evolving family (§3.3). Appendix
Table 15 shows what the model receives in each arm, and Appendix D gives the prompt for each arm.
Arm C builds its identity states in two steps. First, we construct a set of candidate identity states for the population.
For Chameleon we fit a Gaussian mixture model (GMM), a statistical method that finds groups of similar observations in
data without predefined labels. In our case the observations are users’ trait scores, so the groups are users with similar
trait profiles. For the motivational-themes study we write the states from clinical-recruitment literature, independently
of the target study, and check them for circularity. Second, each person’s affinity for each state is matched against the
context to pick one active state. Figures 3 and 4 in the appendix diagram the two pipelines. We fix each specification
before testing it on real data. Any later revision is reported as a separate, documented version (Appendix C).

All arms use the same elicitation format, in which the model gives a short reasoning and then a rating (Appendix C.5),

The two datasets test different parts of the criterion. Chameleon tests the first component (between-person share)
and the third, person-specific signal, directly. The motivational-themes study has one observation per person, so it
cannot test the third component. We compare each arm with the published aggregate coefficients instead, which tests
the second component, whether the system reproduces the real person-context interaction in aggregate.
5.2
A Naturalistic Check: Chameleon
This check asks whether each system reproduces a known pattern in real people, in which most of what varies in their
behavior is within each person, from one situation to the next, and little is between people. The target is the Chameleon
dataset [18], with 1,667 Reddit users and three posts each in different subreddits. Each post is scored on twenty-six trait
dimensions, including the five Big Five personality traits (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism,
and openness), which are the outcomes each simulation must reproduce. The other twenty-one dimensions, from values,
self-determination, and risk-taking scales, describe the person in the persona. In the real data about 1% of the variation
is between people, and almost all of it is within each person across situations (§2.2). This uses a bias-corrected ICC,
which removes the noise that three posts per person add.
On the first component, fixed personas show the opposite of the real pattern (Table 1). The real between-person
share is about 1% (mean ICC 0.013), and Arms A and B place it at 91% and 80%. Arm C is the closest to the real value on
all five traits and on average (0.285), and it recovers extraversion almost exactly (0.023 against 0.019). On the other four
traits it remains above the real value, by 24 to 65 times on agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness, and below
it on neuroticism. On the third component, person-specific signal is near zero for every arm (mean correlation 0.026
for Arm A, 0.025 for Arm B, and 0.004 for Arm C), and only extraversion is significant for all three (Table 1). This is
the pattern Proposition 2 predicts for fixed personas. Their small positive values are what the robustness corollary
(§4.2) expects, since a persona built from scores of the user’s own posts, and subreddits that users chose for themselves,
can carry a little information about that user’s specific responses. With about 4,570 comparisons per trait, the test can
reliably detect a correlation of about 0.04 or more (80% power, Appendix C.1). It therefore rules out a correlation well
above that size in every arm and cannot separate the arms below it, so whether the person-in-context design recovers
person-specific signal remains open.

distrust of medical institutions is documented in this community [5, 29] and could change how a theme lands. We use
its published results as the target.

5.3.1
Setting and Reproduction Target. The study surveyed 829 Black adults. Each saw one of four ad themes (contribu-
tion to science, community benefit, blood-pressure management, financial compensation), a between-subject design.
They rated appeal, willingness to participate, and willingness to recommend on 5-point scales. The target has two parts.
First, no theme has a significant main effect on any outcome (𝑝= .25 to .93). Second, theme and trust interact for the
blood-pressure theme. All six blood-pressure by trust odds ratios are below 1 at both trust extremes relative to neutral,
which means lower odds of a favorable rating. Significance alternates by outcome between the trust side and the distrust
side. The target is this six-coefficient directional pattern, which is stricter than significance at both extremes (Appendix
Table 10 lists the published design and values). For each arm we estimate the same six interactions and compare their
signs with the published ones. The main analysis is a linear model in rating points with the contribution-to-science
theme and neutral trust as baselines, and Appendix Table 11 repeats the comparison under seven analysis specifications.
The theme main effect does not separate the families, but the interaction is informative, since it requires the same
theme to evoke a different response depending on a person-level trait. A fixed persona can reproduce part of this pattern
in aggregate, because the language model links a stated trait to the ad text on its own. It cannot represent an identity
that changes with the theme. The aggregate interaction is therefore a coarse test of whether a system represents the
shift, since it cannot show whether any single simulated person changed.
5.3.2
Materials and Cohort. All three arms see the study’s advertisement text and outcome wording word for word.
Arm C uses six identity states written from the literature (Appendix C.3). The Medical Skeptic state, on which the target
interaction depends, is driven mainly by trust tier (activation probability 0.70 for distrust, 0.35 for neutral, 0.15 for trust),
with capped demographic modifiers that cannot outvote trust. Appendix C.3 gives the calibration history.
Assignment is between-subject, as in the original study. We use 6,632 demographically resampled personas, one
theme each, and all three arms run on identical cells. As §5.1 explains, Claude Haiku 4.5’s training data end in February
2025, thirteen months before the study’s publication, which makes contamination unlikely.
5.3.3
Results. The generative step responds to the active state. In the full run, 479 of 6,632 personas received, by
chance, at least one response under an active Medical Skeptic state and one under their theme’s own state. The paired
within-person difference is large and consistent (−1.76 appeal, −1.24 participate, −2.44 recommend), with the same
direction in 87 to 100% of personas (Appendix Figure 5). A forced, matched 30-per-condition check gives non-overlapping
95% intervals. Switching a persona’s active state reliably changes behavior in the expected direction.

The diagnosis concerns structure. The Medical Skeptic state’s activation floor is the same for all four themes by
design. It can shift the level of activation by theme, but not the slope of the response to trust. Only a theme-dependent
slope can produce an interaction. Without one, the model yields a general main effect, and this limit holds at any
sample size. Arm C also rates the blood-pressure theme lower than the contribution-to-science theme at every trust
level (mean appeal 2.78 against 3.16 at neutral trust; Appendix Table 13), a theme main effect the real study did not find.

the blood-pressure theme (fixed before evaluation, Appendix C.3) does not raise the match count under the contribution-

improves on the domain-general mechanism, and because the amplified theme was chosen knowing where the real
effect lies, it is also a weaker test. The more useful result is the shape of the real effect. All six published coefficients

respondents. A mechanism that moves along one trust axis cannot produce a dip at both extremes (Figure 2).

5.4
Interpretation: What Both Datasets Show
Neither dataset tests whether the person-in-context family is capable of context-dependent behavior, since the natural-
experiment and forced-contrast checks in §5.3.3 show that it is. They test whether this implementation of 𝜋𝐼closes the
gap in practice, which Proposition 2 does not address.
The failure can be diagnosed. The real effect dips at both trust extremes, a shape that no single-axis mechanism can
represent (Figure 2), which is why the theme-amplified refinement produces no gain in Appendix Table 11. Chameleon
adds a complementary view. Its real within-person signal is faint, so it tests whether the implementation’s gain in
aggregate variance carries over to person-specific signal, and so far it does not (§5.2). Appendix Table 3 sets the two
datasets side by side. Together they show what the implementation already does, which is to produce behavior that
follows the active state, and where work remains, in the choice of state and its link to person-specific signal.
6
Discussion
6.1
What the empirical tests show, and what they do not
The proof and the implementation are separate questions. Propositions 1 and 2 (§4) concern the design, and the
experiments concern one implementation of the alternative. The difficulty in those tests concentrates in 𝜋𝐼, the step
that selects the identity state, while the generative step, 𝑓, performs well (§5.4).
Some of what we observe comes from the language model as well as from the design. Ratings follow a stated trait
almost mechanically, and Arm A’s appeal ratings sit close to 2, 3, and 4 for distrust, neutral, and trust in every theme
(Appendix C.4). One added sentence reversed five of the six interaction coefficients (Arm B). And the model responds
strongly and in the expected direction when its active identity state is switched (§5.3.3), so the weak point is choosing
the state. Acting it out works. The theory does not supply these facts about language models as simulators, and they
should be rechecked with other models.
6.2
What this changes for evaluation practice
Persona adherence is the right check for the tasks that fixed-persona and memory-evolving systems were built for,
namely covering varied profiles [21], predicting individual attitudes [25], and simulating sandbox dynamics [24]. It is
the wrong check when such systems are proposed for behavioral simulation, because §3.3 shows that a design can
satisfy persona adherence and still be unable to produce the person-context interaction that behavioral simulation needs.
Any claim of behavioral simulation should report the criterion in §4 directly instead of inferring it from adherence or
demographic accuracy.
The motivational-themes study adds guidance for the one family that can simulate behavior in principle. Because the
failure sits in 𝜋𝐼, auditing the generative step alone checks necessary behavior but not sufficient behavior, so evaluations
should also audit 𝜋𝐼against real population data (Appendix C.3) and should not treat a literature-grounded specification
as self-validating.
6.3
What this changes for identity representation
The fixed-persona design misrepresents groups whose behavior depends on context, and richer personas do not remove
the problem. The distrust example from the introduction applies here, since a fixed persona stores distrust as a static
attribute or leaves it out, and neither represents a state that the situation activates, as with distrust of medical institutions
in the motivational-themes study [5, 29]. §5.3.3 shows the risk is concrete, because our own person-in-context design,
Manuscript submitted to ACM

14
Harry and Feng
built to represent trust as context-dependent, produces a version of the same flattening. The risk therefore appears
whenever a mechanism that selects identity states under-represents how a trait depends on context.
This also moves an accountability question without removing it. A fixed persona’s content can be traced to a stated
source, such as demographic conditioning or a profile instrument. A person-in-context system also needs someone to
specify I𝑝itself (the set of identity states available to each person, §3.2), meaning which identity states exist, what
activates them, and whether a behavior counts as state-typical or person-specific. That choice is an interpretation.
Our own mechanism needed four locked revisions (documented versions, each fixed in writing before it was run
and each prompted by a failed internal check) before it passed those checks, and the first three versions, though
literature-grounded, failed them. The question shifts from what the persona says to who decides what a person’s
possible identity states are.
6.4
Limitations
Data. The 6,632 simulated personas in the motivational-themes study match the published share of each demographic
group, such as age or income, but not how those characteristics combine in real respondents. The Chameleon test uses
the real users, with three posts each (§5.2). Method. The identity states are written by hand, so the results concern this
implementation and say nothing about learned mechanisms (§6.5). In Chameleon, Claude Haiku 4.5 also scores how
well each subreddit matches each identity state (Appendix C), in a separate call from the one that generates behavior,
so shared biases may carry through, a concern raised for LLM-as-judge setups [20], and we did not test for this. In the
motivational-themes study the cue scores were set by the authors, and the Medical Skeptic score was revised after
a pilot (Appendix C.3). The three identity-state panels were isolated agent sessions of Claude Sonnet 5.5, one model
family with no human raters. Their agreement rules out one unlucky sample, but not a bias that all three share. Scope.
The framework models cross-sectional response only, and the between-subject motivational-themes study limits that
test to the aggregate interaction. The proof does not depend on any of these.
6.5
Forward
Three directions follow. First, §5.3.3 motivates activation mechanisms that do not move along a single trust axis, since
the real effect dips at both trust extremes. One outcome can arise from different states, for example institutional distrust
at one extreme and a different skepticism at the other. Second, it is open whether the baseline-plus-activated-states
structure in §3.2 is general or specific to Chameleon. A related test is whether Arm C does best on the trait whose
context cues are clearest, which could explain why it recovers extraversion (§5.2). We have not tested this. Third,
because the difficulty sits in 𝜋𝐼, it could be learned against held-out real data instead of set by hand [41], for example
with an input-driven switching state-space model in which context drives transitions between identity states and a
language model writes the behavior from the current state. Testing it needs longitudinal data that our datasets lack.
Ethical Considerations
Data and human subjects. We collected no new human-subjects data, and both datasets are already published. Chameleon
is a public dataset of pseudonymous trait scores computed from public Reddit posts. It keeps the original post identifiers,
so a record could in principle be linked to a public post. We made no attempt to identify, link, or contact any user,
and we report results only in aggregate. The motivational-themes study contributes its published coefficients, sample
demographics, and survey wording [17]. We used the advertisement text only as a fixed input to language models, and
Manuscript submitted to ACM

15
did not use it to recruit or contact anyone. An institutional review board determined through its self-determination
process that the project is not human-subjects research.
Population construction and the risk of encoding stereotypes. The identity-state specification in §5.3 was built for a
population, Black adults evaluating clinical-trial recruitment materials, for whom misrepresentation has real stakes,
given the documented history of harm and eroded trust around clinical research [3, 29]. Three steps address this. The
state content and demographic modifiers came from the independent health-behavior and clinical-recruitment literature,
without using the target study’s results (Appendix C.3). Three isolated, blind sessions of Claude Sonnet 5.5 checked the
specification without access to the target study’s findings (three sessions of one model, which are not independent
raters; see §6.4). And the paper’s central claim is that institutional distrust in this population is context-activated, so
assigning a static “distrusts the healthcare system” trait to a demographic group is the essentializing move the paper
argues against. This does not remove the risk of misuse, because a published state specification could be separated from
its context-activation framing.
Deployment risk. This work does not support replacing engagement with underrepresented populations by simulation,
and our results are evidence against doing so. A reader could conclude that a validated person-in-context system could
substitute for focus groups, surveys, or community engagement, especially for populations that are costly to recruit.
That is where substitution would do the most harm. Our mechanism was literature-grounded, checked by blind model
sessions, and run on a sample eight times the size of the original study, and it still did not reproduce the published
interaction. This weakens the case for treating current synthetic output as a substitute for the represented population.
These methods suit stress-testing evaluation infrastructure, but decisions about real populations need independent
validation.

B.5
Assumption defensibility across the fixed-persona family
Each assumption is more plausible for some designs than for others. The Key Assumption is strongest for closed-schema
personas built from fixed demographic or categorical fields, which have no channel for person-specific cross-context
deviation. It is weaker for rich, open-ended personas such as interview transcripts, which could incidentally encode
traits correlated with person-specific activation. There Corollary 1 or Corollary 2 carries the argument in place of
the exact-zero result, and the degradation is graceful (small 𝜖1, small bound). The Context Exogeneity Assumption is
strongest for experimentally assigned contexts, such as the between-subject theme randomization in §5.3, and weaker
for observationally sampled ones, such as the subreddit choices in §5.2, where a person’s tendencies could influence
which contexts they appear in. Corollary 2 carries the argument there, and §4.3 states this as a scope condition.

C.1
Full Chameleon Empirical Test
The target population is 1,667 Reddit users (Chameleon [18]), each with three posts across distinct subreddits, scored
on five Big Five personality constructs plus twenty-one auxiliary trait dimensions using SEANCE, a psycholinguistic
text-analysis tool that scores language for psychological content. The real signal is faint. Bias-corrected ICC(1) is
approximately 0.01 to 0.02 across all five constructs, so 98 to 101% of real variance is within-person, with the upper end
above 100% because Neuroticism’s bias-corrected estimate is slightly negative (−0.007, Table 4), a known property of
this estimator when the true value is near zero. Real person-context interaction variance is small in absolute terms,
at 0.072 for extraversion, 0.049 for agreeableness, 0.067 for conscientiousness, 0.022 for neuroticism, and 0.043 for
openness.
Each user’s own three real (user, subreddit) cells are simulated under the three arms of §5.1. Arm A’s persona is a
bio built from the user’s twenty-one non-Big-Five dimensions (Schwartz Values, Self-Determination Theory, and the
DOSPERT risk-taking scale), and the Big Five is excluded because it is the outcome, so that the model cannot echo the
answer. Arm B adds the subreddit name. Arm C adds the active identity state (Appendix C.2). The full-scale run covers
all 1,667 users with two or three replicates per cell, in the hybrid reasoning-and-rating format of Appendix C.5.
Results. ICC. Real ICC clusters in a narrow, near-zero band (0.01 to 0.02). Arms A and B invert this near-completely,
with between-person shares of 83 to 95% for Arm A (mean 0.909) and 60 to 89% for Arm B (mean 0.799) against a real
mean of 0.013, as §3.3 predicts. Arm C’s aggregate ICC (mean 0.285) sits substantially closer to the real value, but the
improvement is not uniform (Table 4). Extraversion is recovered almost exactly, while agreeableness, conscientiousness,
and openness overshoot by roughly 24 to 65-fold, and neuroticism overshoots more strongly in the negative direction.
A person-in-context design that had recovered the real structure would show similarly small ICC on every construct,
whereas Arm C’s values spread widely across constructs.
Person-specific signal. This is the direct test of Proposition 2, computed across approximately 4,568 person-context
comparisons per construct (Table 5). The mean correlation is 0.026 for Arm A, 0.025 for Arm B, and 0.004 for Arm C, near

corollary expects. Arm C’s point estimates are negative on agreeableness and conscientiousness, but its intervals include

Detectability. Each person-specific correlation rests on about 4,570 person-context comparisons per construct, and its
interval comes from a bootstrap that resamples whole users (1,000 resamples). The standard errors implied by those
intervals are 0.012 to 0.016 (mean 0.0145), so an observed correlation must be about 0.033 to 0.045 (mean 0.041) to be

near-zero value does not show that person-specific signal is absent.

Calibration history. Arm C’s mechanism required four locked revisions before passing internal-validity checks, each
triggered by a specific diagnostic failure rather than outcome-directed tuning. The first implementation, a literature-cited
competition among all six identity states, was not self-calibrating, since individually well-grounded modifier magnitudes
let the intended trait be outvoted by unrelated, more prevalent correlates (a college-education correlate common to 76%
of the pilot personas overwhelmed the trust-distrust modifier defining Medical Skeptic). The mechanism that finally
passed instead special-cases Medical Skeptic as a direct function of its own defining trait.

activation spread specifically for the blood-pressure theme. It is reported in the main text as a weaker test than the
domain-general mechanism, since the amplified theme was chosen with knowledge of where the real effect is located.
